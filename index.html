Every week our inbox gets some version of the same message. "I'm eating the same way I always have. I'm moving just as much. So why does my body feel like it's working against me?" It's one of the most common questions we get from women in their late 30s onward, and it turns out there's real research behind it — it just rarely makes it into the conversation.

It's not just willpower

For a long time, the default explanation was some combination of "eat less, move more" and "it's just aging." But over the last decade, researchers studying the gut microbiome have been mapping something more specific: how the community of bacteria in your digestive tract shifts over time, and how that shift affects the way your body handles fiber, blood sugar, and hunger cues.

"Metabolism isn't just calories in, calories out — it's a conversation between your gut, your hormones, and your brain. When that conversation gets quieter, everything downstream gets harder."

— summarizing findings widely discussed in gut-health research literature

Where resistant starch fits in

One area getting particular attention is resistant starch — a type of fiber found in foods like green bananas, potatoes, and chicory root that resists digestion in the small intestine and instead reaches the colon, where gut bacteria ferment it into short-chain fatty acids, including one called butyrate. Several published studies have linked resistant starch intake to modest improvements in insulin sensitivity and appetite regulation.
